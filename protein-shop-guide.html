The one rule that matters more than any product on this list

Protein first, everything else second. If a product is high in protein but also loaded with sugar, it is a dessert wearing a gym vest. Check the protein AND the sugar, not just the protein.

How to actually tell if a snack is worth it (30 seconds, every time)

1. Calories per gram of protein. Divide the calories by the grams of protein. Under 10 is excellent, 10 to 15 is decent, over 20 means you are eating mostly filler to get a small bit of protein.

2. Sugar to protein check. If the sugar (grams) is close to or higher than the protein (grams), walk away. A pot with 15g protein and 18g sugar is a pudding, not a protein food.

3. Cost per gram of protein. Price divided by grams of protein in the pack. UK research on this exact category found the average protein bar costs around 12p per gram of protein, some products nearly double that for the same protein. Yoghurts are consistently the cheapest way to hit your protein target, bars are consistently the most expensive.

Marketing tricks to watch for

"Protein-washing." That is the actual industry term now for slapping a high protein label on something to make it look healthy when the sugar or ultra-processed ingredients tell a different story. A recent UK study of 88 supermarket products found close to 90% exceeded NHS sugar guidance despite being marketed as high protein.

Amino spiking. Mostly a protein powder issue rather than food, but worth knowing. Manufacturers can add cheap amino acids like glycine, taurine or creatine to a powder, which inflates the protein number on the label through the way protein is lab tested, without giving you the same muscle building benefit as real complete protein. If a powder ingredients list has a long string of amino acids before or instead of whey, milk or pea protein, that is a red flag.

The "just about scrapes in" trick. UK law only requires 20% of a product's calories to come from protein for it to be labelled high protein. Some products are built to just clear that bar with flashy packaging doing the rest of the selling. Always check the actual grams, not just the label claim.

Bioavailability, in plain English

Bioavailability just means how well your body can actually absorb and use the protein in a food, not just how many grams are printed on the label. Animal sources, eggs, dairy, meat, fish and whey, are close to fully usable by your body and contain all the essential amino acids your body cannot make itself. Plant sources like beans, oats or pea protein are still genuinely useful but usually rank a bit lower on their own, missing one or two essential amino acids, which is why plant based eaters are told to combine sources, like rice with beans, or oats with pea protein, to get the full amino acid profile in one meal.

For the average person eating a normal mixed diet with any dairy, eggs or meat in it, this is not something to stress over. It mainly matters if you are fully plant based, where the fix is simply combining a couple of protein sources across the day rather than relying on just one.

The rules that apply everywhere

Check sugar, not just protein

A "high protein" label does not mean healthy. Flip the pack over. If sugar is 15g or more per serving, it is a dessert with a gym marketing badge, not a staple food.

Protein cereal is rarely worth it

Most protein cereals add 5 to 8g protein but keep the same sugar as standard cereal. A boiled egg or Greek yoghurt on the side of your normal cereal usually beats buying the protein version outright.

Check the protein source, not just the number on the front

Whey, milk, eggs, meat and fish are complete proteins, they cover all the essential amino acids your body needs in one go. Wheat protein, most protein bagels and bread, and single plant sources like pea or rice protein on their own are incomplete, usually short on one or two essential amino acids (wheat is low in lysine, for example). Not a reason to avoid them, just a reason not to count them as your main protein for a meal. Pair them with eggs, meat, dairy or a mixed plant source instead of relying on them alone.

Protein bagels and bread are a useful swap, with one caveat

They genuinely deliver more protein and less sugar than standard bread, which is a real win. Just remember the point above, the wheat protein in them is incomplete on its own, so treat it as a bonus on top of a proper protein source in the meal, not the meal's main protein.

Plain natural or Greek yoghurt beats flavoured, every time

Add your own fruit and honey. You control the sugar instead of the manufacturer deciding for you, and it is usually cheaper per 100g too.

Protein desserts are a treat, not a food group

Protein ice cream, protein mousse, protein chocolate balls, these exist to make you feel good about a treat. Enjoy them as a treat. Do not build your daily protein target around them.
